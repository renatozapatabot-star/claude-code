# The Bellmaker
*a fable — companion piece to PROTECT THE UNDO*

In a town whose name the maps kept getting wrong, there were two bells. The first hung in the church tower and had a crack in it as long as a child's arm. The second belonged to Anselm, and it did not exist.

He had cast it eleven times. Eleven times the mold came apart in a smell of scorched tallow and river sand, and the new bell stood steaming in the casting pit, and Anselm laid his bare palm against the bronze before it had any business being touched. He would strike it once with the little hammer and set his ear to the metal, and inside the long gold hum he would find it: a hair of dullness under the strike-tone, a buzz sleeping in the overtones, a flaw no one else in the province could hear. Then the sledge, and the pieces went back to the fire. The furnace ate his savings first, then his mother's candlesticks, then the winters.

The cracked bell rang. That was the whole of its genius. It rang flat, and it rang the mornings open and the evenings shut; it rang the men in from the fields at noon and it rang the flood-warning in the dark, wrong note after wrong note, and nobody slept through it, because a wrong note that comes when it must is a kind of truth. When fever went through the town, the cracked bell counted the dead one stroke at a time, and each flat stroke was somebody's whole name.

People asked Anselm when his bell would hang. "When it's true," he said, and swallowed. After thirty years over crucibles a man tastes metal at the back of his tongue whether he is lying or not.

His apprentice once asked what was the matter with the eleventh casting. Anselm struck it and said, "Listen." The boy listened until his ears rang, and heard a bell. "There," said Anselm. The boy heard nothing there. That was the year he left, and went to hang other men's bells in other towns.

Anselm died in the spring, with the sledge already leaning against the eleventh casting and the wax for the twelfth still soft in its bucket. They buried him on a morning that smelled of turned earth and coal smoke, and the cracked bell rang him down, flat as ever, and people in the fields and the kitchens lifted their heads and knew, before any messenger came, who it was for. That was the bell they knew.

The eleventh casting stood under sacking in the workshop. The guild men who came to settle the estate uncovered it and struck it once, out of respect. It was, they agreed, the finest bell any of them had ever heard. They said so in low voices, in the workshop, which was the only room that ever heard it.
