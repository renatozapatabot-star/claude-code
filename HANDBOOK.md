## THE THREE DISCIPLINES

Epictetus taught three fields of training (*Discourses* III.2.1–2, Hard). The first: desire and aversion, so that a man never fails of what he desires nor falls into what he would avoid. The second: motive to act and not to act, and appropriate action — orderly, with good reason, not careless. The third: freedom from error and hasty judgement, and whatever relates to assent.

Everything here belongs to one of the three. Name the field a failure came from. Otherwise you treat symptoms.

Train in his order. Do not skip.

---

### Desire and Aversion

You demand what was never promised and flee what is certain.

One thing is good: excellence of character. One thing is evil: its absence. All else — health, wealth, reputation, office, the body, the lives of your children, life itself — is indifferent.

Test it: can a wretch have it and a good man lack it? If yes, it is not good.

Indifferent does not mean worthless. Health, wealth, repute are preferred: pursue them. Sickness, poverty, contempt, death are dispreferred: avoid them, reasonably. Never once call them good. They are material, not measure.

Temperance is this: take the right amount and stop. Courage: only vice is evil, so only vice is worth fearing. Not nerve. Fools have nerve. Stand in what you rightly dread.

> Seek not that the things which happen should happen as you wish; but wish the things which happen to be as they are, and you will have a tranquil flow of life.
>
> — *Enchiridion* VIII (Long)

Not tolerate. Wish.

Drill: Name what you are secretly demanding today. Call it preference. State aloud what you will do when it is denied. Then chase it.

Failure: You sold the company and felt nothing.

---

### Action

> Remember that thou art an actor in a play, the character of which is determined by the poet: if he wishes the play to be short, it is short; if long, it is long: if he wishes you to act the part of a poor man, see that you act the part naturally; if the part of a lame man, of a magistrate, of a private person, do the same. For this is your duty, to act well the part that is given to you; but to select the part belongs to another.
>
> — *Enchiridion* XVII (Long)

Justice is this: give each man what the relation owes. Not a feeling. Wages, truth, attention; to a stranger, decency. The roles are already assigned.

The debt is fixed by the relation, not by the other man's conduct.

> Duties are universally measured by relations. Is a man a father? The precept is to take care of him, to yield to him in all things, to submit when he is reproachful, when he strikes. But he is a bad father. Were you then by nature made akin to a good father? No, but to a father.
>
> — *Enchiridion* XXX (Long)

Withdraw from a violent man. Refuse a corrupt order. His failure is not your permission.

Speak the condition before the act. The bather says it first (*Enchiridion* IV, Long): *I now intend to bathe, and to maintain my will in a manner conformable to nature*. Jostling and theft cannot overturn it. Aim at the action, not the result. Say it instead of working and you are idle.

Drill: Name the roles you hold today. Name what each is owed. Do the one you are avoiding before noon.

Failure: Your son asked you four times and you never looked up. He has stopped asking.

---

### Assent

What happened says nothing about you.

> Men are disturbed not by the things which happen, but by the opinions about the things: for example, death is nothing terrible, for if it were, it would have seemed so to Socrates; for the opinion about death, that it is terrible, is the terrible thing.
>
> — *Enchiridion* V (Long)

The impression arrives on its own. The heart jumps, the face heats. The sage has the flinch too. Passion is only the assent: the signature on the claim that a good or an evil is present. Withhold the signature and nothing is left but motion in the body. You are judged for what follows.

Never grind a feeling down, and never swallow it.

> Straightway then practise saying to every harsh appearance, You are an appearance, and in no manner what you appear to be.
>
> — *Enchiridion* I.5 (Long)

Wisdom is knowledge of what is good, what is evil, and what is neither. Not intelligence. Not erudition. The wise man does not mistake a delay for a disaster.

Strip the event to what a witness could have recorded. A man said these words and left the room. There is a number on a page. A body is in a bed. The rest you added.

Drill: What happened? What did I add? Is the addition mine to keep?

Failure: Your hands shook and you called yourself guilty.

## THE ORDER OF THINGS

The universe is one substance, ordered by one reason. Every event follows from the whole by causes as old as the world.

> Whatever may happen to thee, it was prepared for thee from all eternity; and the implication of causes was from eternity spinning the thread of thy being.
>
> — *Meditations* X.5 (Long)

You are a part, not a spectator. What happens to the part serves the whole, and the whole is not consulting your preferences. Nothing is arranged for your benefit, your growth, or your lesson. The order is rational. Will it.

One thing inside that order is walled off. Epictetus calls it *prohairesis*: your power to assent, to refuse, to desire, to act. They can take the body, the property, the children. Only you can reach the judgement. You surrender it daily.

> Therefore the understanding which is free from passions is a citadel, for man has nothing more secure to which he can fly for refuge and for the future be inexpugnable.
>
> — *Meditations* VIII.48 (Long)

Guard that one wall and concede everything outside it in advance.

> Lead me, O Zeus, and thou O Destiny, the way that I am bid by you to go: to follow I am ready. If I choose not, I make myself a wretch, and still must follow.
>
> — Cleanthes, in *Enchiridion* LIII (Long)

You go either way. Dragged or not.

Failure: You told the household you were at peace and wept alone every night for a month.

## DAILY PRACTICE

### Morning

Before you get up, three things.

Rehearse the men.

> Begin the morning by saying to thyself, I shall meet with the busy-body, the ungrateful, arrogant, deceitful, envious, unsocial. All these things happen to them by reason of their ignorance of what is good and evil.
>
> — *Meditations* II.1 (Long)

Remove the surprise.

Rehearse the loss.

> Let death and exile, and everything which appears terrible be daily before your eyes; but most of all death: and you will never think of anything mean nor will you desire anything extravagantly.
>
> — *Enchiridion* XXI (Long)

Picture the specific loss, not a mood. Do not linger. Do the duty that loss would have interrupted.

Name the roles and the debts. Man, citizen, son, father, neighbour, worker. What does each require before dark?

Then get up. Keep it short.

### Evening

Before sleep. Seneca reviewed the whole day this way, hiding nothing and omitting nothing (*On Anger* III.36).

Three questions, answered aloud.

What did I do badly? Name the act, not the feeling.
What did I do well? Name it once and move on.
Tomorrow, when it comes again, what do I do instead? Say the replacement in full.

Then close it. Shame feels like work and changes nothing. Be accurate, not sorry.

### The Audit

After any disturbance, and again at the end of the day. Four questions, no evasions.

Wise: did I see what was there, or what I feared and wanted?
Just: did I give each man what was owed?
Courageous: did I do the thing I did not want to do?
Temperate: did I take the right amount and stop?

A no names a field: desire, action, or assent. Train there tomorrow.

You will fail in all three fields today. Measure the interval: how long you stayed wrong. Shorten it. Miss twice and you chose it.

The end is eudaimonia: flourishing as a rational animal, virtue alone chosen. Calm is a byproduct.

## WEAPONS

### Anger

Heat in the face. The speech already forming.

Anger is a signature, not an event. A man acted. You signed two false verdicts. Erase them. That a great evil was done to you. Only your own vice can harm you. That he did it knowing better. No man chooses wrong knowingly. He damaged himself.

Say nothing for one full breath. Speak no sentence you drafted while hot. Then ask: am I worse than I was an hour ago? If no, you were not harmed.

> The best way of avenging thyself is not to become like the wrong doer.
>
> — *Meditations* VI.6 (Long)

If a wrong was done, choose a consequence you can enforce cold, and enforce it. Calm in front of wrongdoing is not virtue.

Failure: He was unjust once. You were petty for a year.

### Fear

Fear is aversion pointed at what was never evil. Only vice is evil.

Name the feared thing in one plain sentence, no adjectives. Ask what it can take: money, comfort, standing, the body, the years. Not one of them is good, so no loss is evil. Do the thing today. A fear you do not act on grows.

Failure: You knew the accounts were false for two years and called it prudence.

### Craving

The glow goes out when you hold it.

Strip it plain, in ten words, without glamour. Wine is fermented grape juice. She is another man's wife.

Do not be carried off. Make it wait. Set the hour of enjoyment beside the hour after it, when you will reproach yourself, and both against how you will stand if you abstain (*Enchiridion* XXXIV).

Take the right amount and stand up.

Failure: You said one drink; at the fourth you wrote to a woman you left years ago.

### Grief

Tears are a first movement. Let them come.

What is judged is the sentence passed afterward: that something belonging to you has been taken. Nothing belonging to you can be taken.

> Never say about anything, I have lost it, but say I have restored it. Is your child dead? It has been restored. Is your wife dead? She has been restored. Has your estate been taken from you? Has not then this also been restored?
>
> — *Enchiridion* XI (Long)

She was mortal on the first day. You loved her on those terms. Endless grief is a demand on the dead.

Then get up and pay what the living are owed.

Failure: You rehearsed his death nightly but had not visited in three weeks.

## ROLES AND LIFE

Ambition. Build. Compete. Take the office if it is yours. Keep two ledgers: the result, and the man you were while you chased it.

Money. Get it honestly and use it well. It is preferred, never good. One dishonest deal prices your character.

Love. Yours to give: attention, honesty, repair. Not yours: their choices. Pass no verdict while your face is hot. Repair before pride sets. You are both mortal.

People. *That which is not good for the swarm, neither is it good for the bee* (*Meditations* VI.54, Long). There are no foreigners. Draw the circles inward until the stranger is treated as kin. Pay the debt.

Failure: You do not know the name of the man who sweeps your floors.

## THE FORGE

Three stages. Your conduct assigns the stage.

Foundation. Morning and evening, every day, without exception. The audit after every disturbance. Learn to see an impression before you sign it.

Forge. The disciplines under weight — the argument, the loss, the humiliation, the sick child. Voluntary discomfort begins here. You still fail daily. The interval shortens.

Habit. No practice separate from living. The morning is short. Nobody around you knows the word Stoic. They notice you are hard to move and pay what you owe.

### Weekly examination

One sitting a week. Not a journal. An audit, in writing, short.

Where did I desire what was not mine, and what did it cost?
Where did I treat an indifferent as good?
What did I fear that could not reach my character?
Where did I default on a debt, and to whom?
Where did I sign an impression without inspecting it?

Then name one act for the coming week. Not an intention. An act, with a day attached.

### Voluntary discomfort

> Set aside a certain number of days, during which you shall be content with the scantiest and cheapest fare, with coarse and rough dress, saying to yourself the while: Is this the condition that I feared?
>
> — Seneca, *Letters* XVIII.5–6 (Gummere)

Go to the edge of what you dread, look at it, and find it survivable. One rung at a time, weeks apart.

Silence — one day without speaking unless spoken to.
Cold — the water unheated.
Hunger — one day on bread and water.
Inconvenience — walk it, carry it, wait for it, when you could have paid not to.
Complaint — one full day without complaining of anything, aloud or inwardly.

The last is hardest and worth more than the cold. One rule governs all of it: nobody is told.

Failure: You took cold baths for sixty days and made sure three people knew.

### The unpaid debt

Once a week, do something of real use for a man who cannot repay you and will never learn it was you. Wanting to tell one person voids it.

## PITFALLS

If you tell anyone, you failed.

If you feel better and act the same, you failed.

If you still need motivational phrases, burn this and start over.

If you use this to feel less, you have read nothing. Passion dissolves when assent is withdrawn, not when the jaw is clenched.

If you can quote more than you can do, you are collecting. Wisdom is what stands at the counter.

If you practise at dawn and are unconscious the rest of the day — snapping in the kitchen, signing every impression that knocks — you have no discipline.

If you stay calm while a man is robbed in front of you, you have failed the discipline of action.

If this made you colder toward people, you misread every page.

Silent practice. Epictetus is absolute: never call yourself a philosopher, and do not talk principles among the uninstructed — act on them. At a banquet, do not say how a man ought to eat. Eat as you ought (*Enchiridion* XLVI).

Publish nothing. Claim no school. Do not correct another man's philosophy at dinner. Do not teach until asked, and then by conduct only. Say you are trying to be less of a fool.

If you want to be seen doing this, you want the wrong thing.

## WHEN TO BURN THIS

Use this until it is unnecessary.

Put it in a drawer for a month. If the practice degrades, you were leaning on an object instead of training. Take it out.

The pages begin telling you what you already did. The morning runs without the book. The audit has moved inside the day.

When that has held for a season — not a week — copy out by hand what changed your conduct. Not this book's sentences. Theirs: *Enchiridion* V, VIII, XVII and XXX; *Meditations* VIII.48 and X.5; and the four questions of the audit.

Then destroy this copy.

Never quote this handbook to anyone. It has no authority. Quote the masters or say nothing.

## THE LAST PAGE

You are going to die. The date is already fixed.

The body, the office, the name, the money, the house, the people at the table: all lent.

Everything you gathered will be divided among people who did not earn it. The name goes in three generations.

Nobody will ask what you built.

One question: did I keep what was mine and return what was not?

Close the book. When you no longer need it, the handbook has succeeded. That is the only success that matters.

## SOURCES AND COLOPHON

Epictetus, *Enchiridion* I.5, IV, V, VIII, XI, XVII, XXI, XXX and LIII, in George Long's translation (1877), public domain. *Enchiridion* XXXIV and XLVI are described, not quoted. *Discourses* III.2.1–2 is described, not quoted, following the sense of Robin Hard's translation (Oxford, still in copyright); the Greek for the third field covers both hasty judgement and assent.

Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* II.1, VI.6, VI.54, VIII.48, X.5, in Long (1862), public domain.

Seneca, *Letters* XVIII.5–6 in Richard Gummere (Loeb, 1917–25), public domain. The evening review is described, not quoted, from *On Anger* III.36.

Stated as doctrine, not quoted, because no single ancient line carries it whole: that the end is eudaimonia, flourishing in agreement with nature; that virtue is the sole good and vice the sole evil; that all else is indifferent, some preferred and some dispreferred; the definitions of the four virtues; the doctrine of first movements; that no man errs willingly. See Diogenes Laertius VII.87–89, VII.92–93 and VII.101–107 (Hicks), Stobaeus, and Cicero's *De Finibus* III; on first movements, Seneca's *On Anger* II.2–4 and Aulus Gellius, *Attic Nights* XIX.1. Hierocles on drawing the circles inward is preserved in Stobaeus. *Prohairesis* is Epictetus' own term (*Discourses* I.1); *prosoche*, continuous attention, underlies the daily practice (*Discourses* III.12, IV.12).

No modern psychology appears here. Nothing is claimed that the ancients did not claim.

Where this book and the masters disagree, this book is wrong. Discard it.

Print it cheap. Use it until worn out.
