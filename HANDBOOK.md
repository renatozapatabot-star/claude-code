# HANDBOOK

---

## PREFACE

Compiled by a student.

What is quoted belongs to Epictetus, Marcus and Seneca. Every unquoted line, the next page included, is the compiler's own, marked at the back.

A handbook. Short enough to carry, crude enough to use while angry.

Read once. Then open only on failure. The next page routes you; read that page alone.

Go to the ancients. Then destroy this.

## WHEN YOU OPEN THIS DISTURBED

Send nothing. Sign nothing. Say nothing. Put the phone in another room. Then answer these.

What am I about to do that cannot be undone?

Has this happened, or am I ahead of it?

What did I add that I cannot document?

Of what I can document, what is mine to answer for?

Name one man I owe here, and the smallest thing I can do for him in ten minutes that I will still endorse tomorrow.

Then do it. Nothing else tonight.

Still disturbed. About to send, sign or walk out — Anger. Drinking — Craving, now. Afraid — Fear. Nothing has happened yet — Waiting. A death, or three in the morning — Grief. Wife, husband, child — Love. Shaking, or the loop will not stop — Assent.

## WEAPONS

### Anger

Heat in the face. The speech already forming.

Anger is not the heat. It is what you sign under it. A man acted; you signed the verdict that an evil was done to you. He can take money, standing, blood. Only your own vice can make you worse.

Speak, send or post no sentence you drafted while hot. One breath if you are sober; if you have been drinking, the unit is not a breath but the night.

Then ask: am I worse than I was an hour ago? If no, you were not corrupted; count the rest as damage. If yes, you are the damage. Decide nothing until it passes.

Your hands may shake for hours. That is aftermath, not fresh vice. You answer for what you signed.

> The best way of avenging thyself is not to become like the wrong doer.
>
> — *Meditations* VI.6 (Long)

Correcting a false record is justice, not vanity. State the documentable fact once, in writing, to the same people, without characterizing the man. Whether they believe you is not yours.

If a wrong was done, choose a consequence you can enforce cold, and enforce it. Calm in front of wrongdoing is not virtue.

Failure: He was unjust once. You were petty for a year.

### Fear

Fear is aversion pointed at what cannot make you evil. Only vice can. If nothing has happened yet, this is not your page — Waiting.

Name the feared thing in one plain sentence, no adjectives. When it threatens money, comfort or standing, count what it can take: real losses, every one — and none of them your character. When it threatens the body or the years, do not count. Go straight to the duty.

Do today what justice required and fear postponed. A fear you do not act on grows.

Failure: You knew the accounts were false for two years and called it prudence.

### Craving

If you have been drinking: assent to nothing, decide nothing, send nothing, leave nothing. Put the phone in another room. The audit is tomorrow's work.

The glow goes out when you hold it.

Strip it plain, in ten words, without glamour. Wine is fermented grape juice. She is another man's wife.

Do not be carried off. Make it wait. Set the hour of enjoyment beside the hour after it, when you will reproach yourself, and both against how you will stand if you abstain (*Enchiridion* XXXIV).

Take the measure the occasion allows, or none, and grip nothing.

Failure: You said one drink; at the fourth you wrote to a woman you left years ago.

### Waiting

Nothing has been done to you. You are doing it to yourself in advance.

Fix the hour you will know. Everything before it is your manufacture, not the event.

Rehearsal is not preparation. You pay it once if it comes; do not pay it forty times first. Skip the morning rehearsal of loss until it is over.

The interval is the only fact you hold. Name what it requires — the work, the people in the house, the sleep — and do that.

Failure: You spent the four days before the result dying of it. It came back clean, and the four days were gone.

### Grief

If nothing has been taken yet, this is not your page — Waiting.

Tears are a first movement — the body's shock, which no man chooses. Let them come, for as long as they come. You withdraw the assent; the body quiets when it quiets. Neither is done by clenching the jaw.

What you can still get wrong is the sentence passed afterward: that something of yours was taken. Nothing truly yours can be taken.

> Never say about any thing, I have lost it, but say I have restored it. Is your child dead? It has been restored. Is your wife dead? She has been restored. Has your estate been taken from you? Has not then this also been restored?
>
> — *Enchiridion* XI (Long)

He was mortal on the first day. That was the arrangement, whether or not you agreed to it.

Months in, when the tears have become a rite, ask whether you are grieving him or paying a debt.

At three in the morning nothing is owed and nothing can be paid. The hour is not a failure; calling it one is a judgement you added. Write what the morning requires, then put this down.

Otherwise, get up and pay what the living are owed.

Failure: Three weeks after the funeral you had still not called his sister.

## ROLES AND LIFE

Love. Pass no verdict while your face is hot. Repair before pride sets. You are both mortal. Yours to give: attention, honesty, repair. Not yours: their choices.

Ambition. Build. Compete. Take the office if it is yours. Keep two ledgers: the result, and the man you were while you chased it.

Money. Get it honestly, use it well. Preferred, never good. One dishonest deal prices your character.

People. *That which is not good for the swarm, neither is it good for the bee* (*Meditations* VI.54, Long). Draw the circles inward until the stranger is treated as kin.

Failure: You have passed the man who sweeps your floors for three years and never asked his name.

## THE THREE DISCIPLINES

Epictetus taught three fields of training (*Discourses* III.2.1–2, Hard). First: desire and aversion, so that a man never fails of what he desires nor falls into what he would avoid. Second: motive to act and not to act — appropriate action, orderly and reasoned. Third: freedom from hasty judgement, and whatever relates to assent.

Name the field a failure came from. Otherwise you treat symptoms. Train in his order.

---

### Desire and Aversion

You demand what was never promised and flee what is certain.

One thing is good: excellence of character. One thing is evil: its absence. All else — health, wealth, reputation, office, the body, the lives of your children, life itself — is indifferent.

Test it: can a wretch have it and a good man lack it? If yes, it is not good.

Indifferent does not mean unreal. Violence breaks bone, fraud takes the money, the grave takes her — and none of it can make you worse without your consent. Health and repute are preferred: pursue them. Sickness, poverty and death are dispreferred: avoid them, reasonably. Never call them good — material, not measure.

Temperance is measure: sometimes abstain, sometimes take part wholly, and grip nothing. Courage is knowing what must be faced and what endured, and facing it. Not nerve. Fools have nerve. Stand where reason and justice put you, afraid or not.

> Seek not that the things which happen should happen as you wish; but wish the things which happen to be as they are, and you will have a tranquil flow of life.
>
> — *Enchiridion* VIII (Long)

Not tolerate. Wish.

Drill: Name what you are secretly demanding today. Call it preference. Say aloud what you will do when it is denied. Then chase it.

Failure: Nine years on a thing that could not repay you. You sold it, felt nothing, and called that mastery.

---

### Action

Justice is this: give each man what the relation owes. Not a feeling. Not a cause. Wages, truth, attention; to a stranger, decency. The roles are already assigned; selecting the part belongs to another (*Enchiridion* XVII).

The debt is fixed by the relation, not by the other man's conduct. Withdraw from a violent man. Refuse a corrupt order. His failure is not your permission, and yours is not excused by his.

> Duties are universally measured by relations. Is a man a father? The precept is to take care of him, to yield to him in all things, to submit when he is reproachful, when he inflicts blows. But suppose that he is a bad father. Were you then by nature made akin to a good father? No; but to a father.
>
> — *Enchiridion* XXX (Long)

Speak the condition before the act: *I now intend to bathe, and to maintain my will in a manner conformable to nature* (*Enchiridion* IV, Long). Jostling and theft cannot overturn it. Aim at the action, not the result.

Drill: Name the roles you hold today, and what each is owed. Do the one you are avoiding before noon.

Failure: Your son asked you four times and you never looked up. He has stopped asking.

---

### Assent

What happens to you does not decide your character. What you choose next reveals it.

> Men are disturbed not by the things which happen, but by the opinions about the things: for example, death is nothing terrible, for if it were, it would have seemed so to Socrates; for the opinion about death, that it is terrible, is the terrible thing.
>
> — *Enchiridion* V (Long)

The impression arrives on its own. The heart jumps, the face heats. The sage has the flinch too: the first shock, which no man chooses, is not yet the passion (*On Anger* II.2–4). The passion is an impulse run past reason, carrying a verdict — an evil is here, something must be done now. It wants your signature.

Refuse it and the body does not stop. Shaking can run for hours, grief for months. That is aftermath, not fresh vice. You answer for what you signed, and for what you did while it wore off.

Do not crush the feeling and do not swallow it. Feel it entirely and act on none of it.

Wisdom is knowledge of what is good, what is evil, and what is neither. The wise man does not mistake a delay for a disaster, nor think a man chooses wrong knowing it is wrong: the one who injured you mistook it for good, and damaged himself.

Strip the event to what a witness could have recorded. A man said these words and left the room. There is a number on a page. A body is in a bed. The rest you added.

Drill: What happened? What did I add? Is the addition mine to keep?

Failure: Your hands shook and you called yourself guilty.

## THE ORDER OF THINGS

The universe is one substance, ordered by one reason.

> Whatever may happen to thee, it was prepared for thee from all eternity; and the implication of causes was from eternity spinning the thread of thy being, and of that which is incident to it.
>
> — *Meditations* X.5 (Long)

You are a part, not a spectator, and the whole is not consulting you. Will it.

One thing inside it is walled off. Epictetus calls it *prohairesis*: your power to assent, to refuse, to desire, to act. They can take the body, the property, the children. Only you can reach the judgement, and you surrender it daily. Guard that wall and concede everything outside it in advance. You go either way, dragged or not.

Failure: You wept nightly for a month; no fault there. You told the household nothing was wrong. Now they know you lie to them.

## DAILY PRACTICE

### Morning

Before you get up, three things.

Rehearse the men.

> Begin the morning by saying to thyself, I shall meet with the busy-body, the ungrateful, arrogant, deceitful, envious, unsocial. All these things happen to them by reason of their ignorance of what is good and evil. But I who have seen the nature of the good that it is beautiful, and of the bad that it is ugly, and the nature of him who does wrong, that it is akin to me, not only of the same blood or seed, but that it participates in the same intelligence and the same portion of the divinity, I can neither be injured by any of them, for no one can fix on me what is ugly, nor can I be angry with my kinsman, nor hate him. For we are made for co-operation, like feet, like hands, like eyelids, like the rows of the upper and lower teeth. To act against one another then is contrary to nature; and it is acting against one another to be vexed and to turn away.
>
> — *Meditations* II.1 (Long)

The surprise goes; the debt stays. They are limbs of the same body. Do the work they make harder.

Rehearse the loss (*Enchiridion* XXI). Once, cold, by choice: picture the specific loss, not a mood, then do the duty it would have interrupted. If it starts without you, repeats and ends in nothing, that is not this practice but the passion wearing its clothes. Already gripped by a loss that has not come? Skip it.

Name the roles and the debts. Son, father, neighbour, worker, citizen. What does each require before dark?

Then get up. Keep it short.

### Evening

Before sleep, aloud. Hide nothing (*On Anger* III.36).

What did I do badly? Name the act, not the feeling.
What did I do well? Name it once and move on.
When it comes again tomorrow, what do I do instead? Say the replacement in full.

Then close it. Shame feels like work and changes nothing. Be accurate, not sorry.

### The Audit

After any disturbance, and again at day's end.

Wise: did I see what was there, or what I feared and wanted?
Just: did I give each man what was owed?
Courageous: did I do what reason and justice required, despite the fear?
Temperate: did I take what the occasion asked — all of it, or none — and grip nothing?

A no names a field: desire, action, or assent. Train there tomorrow.

You will fail in all three fields today. Measure the interval: how long you stayed wrong. Shorten it.

The end is eudaimonia: flourishing as a rational animal, virtue alone chosen. Calm is a byproduct.

You are not emptied. Craving becomes wish, fear becomes caution, pleasure becomes joy — the good feelings (*Diogenes Laertius* VII.116). What is left when passion goes is not nothing.

## THE FORGE

Three stages. Your conduct assigns the stage.

Foundation. Morning and evening, every day. The audit after every disturbance.

Forge. The disciplines under weight — the argument, the loss, the humiliation, the sick child. Voluntary discomfort begins here. You still fail daily. The interval shortens.

Habit. No practice separate from living. Nobody around you knows the word Stoic. They notice you are hard to move and pay what you owe.

### Weekly examination

One sitting a week. An audit, written, short.

Where did I desire what was not mine, and what did it cost?
Where did I treat an indifferent as good?
What did I fear that could not reach my character?
Where did I default on a debt, and to whom?
Where did I sign an impression without inspecting it?

Then name one act for the week. Not an intention. An act, with a day attached.

### Voluntary discomfort

> Set aside a certain number of days, during which you shall be content with the scantiest and cheapest fare, with coarse and rough dress, saying to yourself the while: Is this the condition that I feared?
>
> — Seneca, *Letters* XVIII.5–6 (Gummere)

Go to the edge of what you dread, look at it, find it survivable. One rung at a time, weeks apart.

Silence — one day without speaking unless spoken to.
Cold — the water unheated.
Complaint — one full day without complaining, aloud or inwardly.

The last is hardest. One rule governs all: nobody is told.

Failure: You took cold baths for sixty days and made sure three people knew.

### The unpaid debt

Once a week, do something of real use for a man who cannot repay you, and arrange that he never learns. Catch yourself composing the account and you have taken your payment.

## PITFALLS

If being seen was the reward, you trained reputation, not character.

If you feel better and act the same, you failed.

You came here to feel less. That is honest, and it is not what this trains. Relief comes as a byproduct or not at all.

If you can quote more than you can do, you are collecting. Wisdom is what stands at the counter.

If you practise at dawn and are unconscious by noon, you have no discipline.

If you stay calm while a man is robbed in front of you, you have failed the discipline of action.

If this made you colder toward people, you misread every page.

Silent practice. Epictetus is plain: never call yourself a philosopher, and do not talk principles among the uninstructed. At a banquet, do not say how a man ought to eat. Eat as you ought (*Enchiridion* XLVI).

Claim no school. If asked, answer briefly and go back to work. Say you are trying to be less of a fool.

## WHEN TO BURN THIS

Use this until it is unnecessary.

Put it in a drawer for a month. If the practice degrades, you were leaning on the object. Take it out.

The pages begin telling you what you already did. The morning runs without the book. The audit has moved inside the day.

When that has held for a season — not a week — copy out by hand what changed your conduct. Not this book's sentences. Theirs: *Enchiridion* V, VIII, XVII and XXX; *Meditations* II.1 and X.5; and the four questions of the audit.

Then destroy this copy.

It has no authority. Quote the masters or say nothing.

---

## THE LAST PAGE

Read this in a calm hour, not a frightened one.

You are going to die. The date is already fixed.

The body, the office, the name, the money, the house, the people at the table: all lent.

What you built passes to others. The name goes in three generations. Build well anyway.

Whether anyone remembers the work was never yours. How you did it, and why, was.

One question: did I keep what was mine and return what was not?

Close the book. When you no longer need it, the handbook has succeeded.

## SOURCES AND COLOPHON

Epictetus, *Enchiridion* IV, V, VIII, XI and XXX, in George Long's translation (1877), public domain. *Enchiridion* I.5, XVII, XXI, XXXIV and XLVI are cited, not quoted, as is *Discourses* III.2.1–2, following the sense of Robin Hard's translation (Oxford, still in copyright).

Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* II.1, VI.6, VI.54 and X.5, in Long (1862), public domain.

Seneca, *Letters* XVIII.5–6 in Richard Gummere (Loeb, 1917–25), public domain. The evening review (*On Anger* III.36) and the first movements (*On Anger* II.2–4) are described, not quoted.

Stated as doctrine, not quoted, because no single ancient line carries it whole: that the end is eudaimonia; that virtue is the sole good and vice the sole evil — a claim about corruption, not about injury; that all else is indifferent, some preferred, some dispreferred; the four virtues; first movements; the good feelings; that no man errs willingly. See Diogenes Laertius VII.87–89, VII.92–93, VII.101–107 and VII.116 (Hicks), Stobaeus (also Hierocles on drawing the circles inward). *Prohairesis* is Epictetus' own (*Discourses* I.1).

No modern psychology appears here. The quotations and the governing doctrines are ancient. The arrangement, the drills, the examples and every imperative — the five questions, the three stages, the one breath, the weekly debt, the ladder, the burning — are the compiler's applications, and carry no ancient authority.

Where this book and the masters disagree, this book is wrong. Discard it.

Print it cheap. Use it until worn out.
