# AFTER THE SLAVE

### Notes on the Three Fields

---

## PREFACE

Compiled by a student. These are not my words.

What is quoted belongs to Epictetus, to Marcus, to Seneca, and to the men they quote. What is not quoted is doctrine, stated plainly and marked as such at the back. Nothing here is new. Nothing here was meant to be.

This is a manual, not a book. It is short so you can carry it, crude so you can use it while angry.

Read it once to learn what is in it. After that, open it only when you have failed. Name the field the failure came from — desire, action, or assent — and read that page alone. Then shut it.

Then go to the ancients. This exists to send you to them and then to be destroyed.

---

## THE THREE DISCIPLINES

Epictetus did not teach maxims. He taught three fields of training (*Discourses* III.2.1–2, Hard). The first concerns desires and aversions, so that a man may never fail to get what he desires nor fall into what he would avoid. The second concerns motives to act and not to act, and appropriate action generally: that he act in an orderly manner and with good reason, not carelessly. The third concerns the avoidance of error and hasty judgement, and in general whatever relates to assent.

Everything here belongs to one of the three. Name the field a failure came from, or you will treat symptoms for forty years and call it philosophy.

Train in his order. Do not skip to the field that flatters you.

---

### Desire and Aversion

You suffer because you demand what the gods never promised and flee what they guaranteed.

One thing is good: excellence of character. One thing is evil: its absence. All else — health, wealth, reputation, office, the body, the lives of your children, even life itself — is indifferent.

Do not soften it. Test any candidate: can it be present in a wretch and absent in a good man? Money can, health can, long life can, the love of your children can. Then none of them is good.

Now the qualification, and not one word earlier. Indifferent does not mean worthless. Life, health, strength, wealth, good repute are preferred: pursue them. Sickness, poverty, contempt, death are dispreferred: avoid them, reasonably. Chase them with everything in you and never once call them good. They are the material of the work, not the measure.

Two virtues govern this field. Temperance: take the right amount and stop. It forbids no pleasure; it forbids being commanded by one. Courage: since only vice is evil, only vice is worth fearing. Not nerve, not daring — fools have both. It is standing in what you rightly dread.

> Seek not that the things which happen should happen as you wish; but wish the things which happen to be as they are, and you will have a tranquil flow of life.
>
> — *Enchiridion* VIII (Long)

It does not say tolerate what you cannot change. It says wish it. Meet the dreaded event as a man who ordered it.

Drill: Name what you are secretly demanding today. Call it preference. State aloud what you will do if it is denied. Then chase it. The outcome cannot touch you.

Failure: You sold the company at noon and felt nothing. By Thursday you were hunting the next number. Nine years bought a figure and did not touch what you are.

---

### Action

You did not choose your part. You choose only how it is played.

> Remember that thou art an actor in a play, the character of which is determined by the poet: if he wishes the play to be short, it is short; if long, it is long: if he wishes you to act the part of a poor man, see that you act the part naturally; if the part of a lame man, of a magistrate, of a private person, do the same. For this is your duty, to act well the part that is given to you; but to select the part belongs to another.
>
> — *Enchiridion* XVII (Long)

Justice governs this field: knowledge of what is owed to each. Not fairness as a feeling. Not a cause. To this man his wage, to this one the truth, to this one your attention, to the stranger the treatment due a human being. Duty is not a mood. It is the content of a role, and the roles are already assigned.

The debt is fixed by the relation, not by the other man's conduct.

> Duties are universally measured by relations. Is a man a father? The precept is to take care of him, to yield to him in all things, to submit when he is reproachful, when he strikes. But he is a bad father. Were you then by nature made akin to a good father? No, but to a father.
>
> — *Enchiridion* XXX (Long)

Withdraw from a violent man. Refuse a corrupt order. You may not convert his failure into permission for yours.

Every act goes out under a condition spoken in advance. Epictetus makes the bather say it first (*Enchiridion* IV, Long): *I now intend to bathe, and to maintain my will in a manner conformable to nature*. Jostling and theft cannot overturn it. Aim at the action, not the result. The condition falls on the outcome, never on the effort. Say it instead of working and you are idle, with a quotation.

Drill: Name the roles you hold today. Name what each is owed. Do the one you are avoiding before noon.

Failure: Your son asked you a question and you said "one second" four times without looking up. He has not asked since. You played the role for the room and lost the only witness.

### Assent

Nothing that has happened to you has yet said anything about you.

> Men are disturbed not by the things which happen, but by the opinions about the things: for example, death is nothing terrible, for if it were, it would have seemed so to Socrates; for the opinion about death, that it is terrible, is the terrible thing.
>
> — *Enchiridion* V (Long)

The impression arrives on its own. The heart jumps, the face heats, the mind offers its picture of ruin. That is not passion. The Stoics called these first movements, and the sage has them too.

Passion is assent to the impression that something good or evil is present. Withhold the signature and there is no passion, only motion in the body. You are not judged for the flinch. You are judged for what follows.

Never grind a feeling down, and never swallow it. There is nothing to swallow if you did not sign.

> Straightway then practise saying to every harsh appearance, You are an appearance, and in no manner what you appear to be.
>
> — *Enchiridion* I.5 (Long)

Wisdom governs this field: knowledge of what is good, what is evil, and what is neither. It is not intelligence, not erudition, not a store of quotations, and a wise man may be slow and unread. He does not mistake a delay for a disaster or a promotion for a good.

Strip the event to what a witness could have recorded. A man said these words and left the room. There is a number on a page. A body is in a bed. Everything past that is commentary you wrote and read back to yourself as news.

Drill: Three questions, in order. What happened? What did I add? Is the addition mine to keep? Withhold assent until you can answer the third without flinching.

Failure: Your hands shook, so you concluded you were a fraud and spoke like one for fifty minutes. You convicted yourself on the evidence of your own pulse.

## THE ORDER OF THINGS

Cut this page and what remains is comfort.

The universe is one substance, ordered by one reason. Every event follows from the whole by causes as old as the world.

> Whatever may happen to thee, it was prepared for thee from all eternity; and the implication of causes was from eternity spinning the thread of thy being.
>
> — *Meditations* X.5 (Long)

You are a part, not a spectator. What happens to the part serves the whole, and the whole is not consulting your preferences. Nothing is arranged for your benefit, your growth, or your lesson. Read that again. If the order were blind, willing it would be a lie. Because the order is rational, willing it is not surrender. It is agreement with what is.

Inside that order one thing is walled off. Epictetus calls it *prohairesis*: your power to assent, to refuse, to desire, to act. The body can be chained, the property seized, the name blackened, the children buried. The judgement cannot be reached except by you, and you surrender it constantly, for free.

> Therefore the understanding which is free from passions is a citadel, for man has nothing more secure to which he can fly for refuge and for the future be inexpugnable.
>
> — *Meditations* VIII.48 (Long)

Guard that one wall and concede everything outside it in advance.

> Lead me, O Zeus, and thou O Destiny, the way that I am bid by you to go: to follow I am ready. If I choose not, I make myself a wretch, and still must follow.
>
> — Cleanthes, in *Enchiridion* LIII (Long)

You are going where it goes either way. The only question is whether you are dragged.

Failure: The word came from the physician and you said everything happens for a reason. You told the household you were at peace and wept alone every night for a month. You did not will it. You narrated it. Resignation under a fine sentence is still resignation. The sentence is not even Stoic.

## DAILY PRACTICE

### Morning

Before your feet are on the floor, three things.

Rehearse the men.

> Begin the morning by saying to thyself, I shall meet with the busy-body, the ungrateful, arrogant, deceitful, envious, unsocial. All these things happen to them by reason of their ignorance of what is good and evil.
>
> — *Meditations* II.1 (Long)

You are not predicting; you are removing surprise, which is their only weapon.

Rehearse the loss.

> Let death and exile, and everything which appears terrible be daily before your eyes; but most of all death: and you will never think of anything mean nor will you desire anything extravagantly.
>
> — *Enchiridion* XXI (Long)

Picture the specific loss, not a mood. Do not linger. Then do the duty that loss would have interrupted. Rehearsal that does not end in an act is not practice.

Name the roles and the debts. Man, citizen, son, father, neighbour, worker. What does each require of you before dark?

Then get up. A morning practice that runs long is a man hiding from the day inside his philosophy.

### Evening

Before sleep. Seneca reviewed the whole day this way, hiding nothing from himself and omitting nothing (*On Anger* III.36).

Three questions, answered aloud.

What did I do badly? Name the act, not the feeling.
What did I do well? Name it once and move on.
Tomorrow, when it comes again, what do I do instead? Say the replacement in full.

Then close it. A man who lies awake prosecuting himself has only found a new way to be governed by yesterday. Shame feels like work and changes nothing. You are not here to be sorry. You are here to be accurate.

### The Audit

Run it after any disturbance, and again at the end of the day. Four questions, no evasions.

Wise: did I see what was there, or what I feared and wanted?
Just: did I give each man what was owed?
Courageous: did I do the thing I did not want to do?
Temperate: did I take the right amount and stop?

A no is not an occasion for feeling. It is a location: desire, action, or assent. Train there tomorrow.

You will fail in all three fields today. What is measured is the interval — how long you stayed wrong before you came back. Shorten the interval. That is the entire art. Miss once and you missed. Miss twice and you chose it.

## WEAPONS

### Anger

Heat in the face, and the speech already drafting itself in your mouth.

Anger is a signature, not an event. A man acted, and you signed two verdicts: that a great evil had been done to you, and that he did it knowing better. Both halves are false. He cannot harm you; only your own vice can. And no man chooses wrong knowingly — he took what appeared good to him and was wrong about it. He damaged himself and handed you material.

Say nothing for one full breath. Speak no sentence you drafted while hot. Then ask the only question: am I worse than I was an hour ago? If no, you were not harmed, whatever you lost.

> The best way of avenging thyself is not to become like the wrong doer.
>
> — *Meditations* VI.6 (Long)

If a wrong was done, choose a consequence you can enforce cold, and enforce it. Justice is owed, and calm in the presence of wrongdoing is not a virtue but the absence of one.

Failure: He lied to your superiors about your work and you were passed over. He degraded himself in one meeting. You spent eight months rehearsing a speech to an empty room, sleeping badly, turning small talk into evidence. He was unjust once. You were petty two hundred times, and you called it being wronged.

### Fear

Fear is aversion pointed at what was never evil. Only vice is evil. Then what are you fleeing?

Name the feared thing in one plain sentence, with no adjectives. Ask what it can take from you: money, comfort, standing, the body, the years. Not one item on that list is good, therefore not one loss is evil. Then do the thing today. A fear that is named and not answered with an act before dark is being fed.

Failure: You have known for two years that your partner is lying about the accounts. You have debts and a child in school, so you told yourself you were being prudent and waiting for proof. You were being a coward, and you have had seven hundred days to notice. Every month of silence costs more than the debt you fear.

### Craving

The glow around a thing, which goes out the moment you hold it.

Strip it plain, in ten words, without glamour. Wine is fermented grape juice. She is another man's wife.

Epictetus gives the procedure for pleasure. Do not be carried off by it. Let the thing wait for you; grant yourself a delay. Then set the hour of enjoyment beside the hour after it, when you will reproach yourself, and set both against how you will stand if you abstain (*Enchiridion* XXXIV).

You are not forbidden the thing. You are forbidden to be commanded by it. Take the right amount and stand up while there is still food on the table.

Failure: You said one drink. At the fourth you sent word to a woman you left years ago and told yourself it had been a heavy week. You were not overwhelmed by anything. Your appetite asked a small question and you said yes four times, then produced a reason afterward, as slaves do.

### Grief

Let the wave through. The tears are a first movement, and you are not judged for them.

What is judged is the sentence passed afterward: that something belonging to you has been taken. Nothing belonging to you can be taken.

> Never say about anything, I have lost it, but say I have restored it. Is your child dead? It has been restored. Is your wife dead? She has been restored. Has your estate been taken from you? Has not then this also been restored?
>
> — *Enchiridion* XI (Long)

She was mortal on the first day, and you loved her on those terms or you were loving something you invented. Grief that will not end is a demand still being made of the dead.

Then get up and pay what the living are owed.

Failure: You rehearse your father's death for forty minutes most nights, feel the appropriate weight, and call this practice. You have not been to see him in three weeks. You replaced a duty you could still perform with a performance of grief you enjoy.

## ROLES AND LIFE

Ambition. Build. Compete. Take the office if it is yours to take. Keep two ledgers: the result, and the man you were while you chased it. Only the second is real. Lose it and the first is a number.

Money. Get it honestly and use it well. It is preferred, never good. A man who will do one dishonest thing for money has priced his character, and the price is known.

Love. Yours to give: attention, honesty, repair. Never yours: their years, their choices, their staying. Pass no verdict while your face is hot. Repair before pride sets. You are both mortal. Nobody promised tomorrow.

People. *That which is not good for the swarm, neither is it good for the bee* (*Meditations* VI.54, Long). Justice has no border and no price. Draw the outer circles inward: the stranger treated as the neighbour, the neighbour as kin. Learn the name of whoever cleans the room you work in. That is not a kindness. It is a debt you have not paid in years.

Failure: You are generous to everyone inside your street and your income. The man who has swept your floors for four years does not know your name, and you do not know his. Your justice stops precisely where usefulness stops. That is not justice. It is tribe loyalty with better manners.

## THE FORGE

Three stages. You do not choose your stage; your conduct assigns it.

Foundation. Morning and evening, every day, without exception. The audit after every disturbance. You are not training yet, only learning to see an impression before you sign it, and that takes months.

Forge. The disciplines under weight — the argument, the loss, the humiliation, the sick child. Voluntary discomfort begins here. You still fail daily. The interval shortens.

Habit. No practice separate from living. The morning is short because there is nothing left to learn. Nobody around you knows the word Stoic. They notice only that you are difficult to move and that you pay what you owe. You no longer report progress, even privately. Nobody is left outside the work to be impressed.

### Weekly examination

One sitting a week. Not a journal. An audit, in writing, in the fewest words that are true.

Where did I desire what was not mine, and what did it cost?
Where did I treat an indifferent as good?
What did I fear that could not reach my character?
Where did I default on a debt, and to whom?
Where did I sign an impression without inspecting it?

Then name one act for the coming week. Not an intention. An act, with a day attached.

### Voluntary discomfort

> Set aside a certain number of days, during which you shall be content with the scantiest and cheapest fare, with coarse and rough dress, saying to yourself the while: Is this the condition that I feared?
>
> — Seneca, *Letters* XVIII.5–6 (Gummere)

The purpose is a verdict, not a sensation. Go to the edge of what you dread, look at it, and find it survivable. Climb one rung at a time, weeks apart.

Silence — one day without speaking unless spoken to.
Cold — the water unheated, without theatre.
Hunger — one day on bread and water.
Inconvenience — walk it, carry it, wait for it, when you could have paid not to.
Complaint — one full day without complaining of anything, aloud or inwardly.

The last is hardest and worth more than the cold. One rule governs all of it: nobody is told. A practice with witnesses is not a practice. It is a performance you have not yet admitted to.

Failure: You took cold baths for sixty days and mentioned it on day one, day nine and day sixty. You now have a slightly higher tolerance for cold water and a considerably higher appetite for being seen enduring things. One of those you already had far too much of.

### The unpaid debt

Once a week, do something of real use for a person who cannot repay you and will never learn it was you. If you want to tell one person, it does not count. That is the whole test.

## PITFALLS

If you tell anyone, you failed.

If you feel better and act the same, you failed. Calm is not the aim. Character is.

If you still need motivational phrases, burn this and start over.

If you use this to feel less, you have read nothing. The work is to judge correctly. Passion dissolves when assent is withdrawn, not when the jaw is clenched.

If you can quote more than you can do, you are collecting. You have read three books this year and can recite Marcus from memory. Then you were kept waiting an hour and told a clerk she was incompetent. Wisdom is not what you can recite in a quiet room. It is what stands at the counter.

If you practise at dawn and are unconscious the rest of the day — snapping in the kitchen, signing every impression that knocks — you do not have a discipline. You have a dawn.

If you stay calm while a man is robbed in front of you, you have achieved nothing. You have failed the discipline of action.

If this made you colder toward people, you misread every page. Indifference is a verdict about outcomes. It was never permission to care less.

Silent practice. Epictetus is absolute: never call yourself a philosopher, and do not talk principles among the uninstructed — act on them. At a banquet, do not say how a man ought to eat. Eat as you ought (*Enchiridion* XLVI).

Publish nothing. Claim no school. Do not correct another man's philosophy at dinner. Do not teach until asked. When asked, teach by conduct and nothing else. Say you are trying to be less of a fool.

If you want to be seen doing this, you want the wrong thing, and you will get it.

## WHEN TO BURN THIS

Use this until it is unnecessary.

Put it in a drawer for a month. If the practice degrades, you were leaning on an object instead of training. Take it out and be less impressed with yourself.

The pages begin telling you what you already did. The morning runs without the book. The audit has moved inside the day.

When that has held for a season — not a week — copy out by hand what changed your conduct. Not this book's sentences. Theirs: *Enchiridion* V, VIII, XVII and XXX; *Meditations* VIII.48 and X.5; and the four questions of the audit. That is the whole of it. The rest was scaffolding. Knock it down.

Then destroy this copy.

Never quote this manual to anyone. It has no authority. Quote the masters, or say nothing and be evidence.

## THE LAST PAGE

You are going to die. Not as a fact you accept — as a date already fixed in the causes.

The body was borrowed. The office was borrowed. The name, the money, the house, the people at the table: all lent, all being called in, some tonight.

Everything you gathered will be divided among people who did not earn it. The name goes in three generations, sooner if you were ordinary, and you were.

You will not be asked what you built. You will not be asked who wept.

When the last breath comes, let the only question be: Did I keep what was mine and return what was not?

Everything else is nothing.

Close the book.

Stated as doctrine rather than quoted, because no single ancient line carries it whole: that virtue is the sole good and vice the sole evil; that all else is indifferent, some preferred and some dispreferred; the definitions of the four virtues; the doctrine of first movements; that no man errs willingly. For these see Diogenes Laertius VII.92–93 and VII.101–107 (Hicks), Stobaeus, and Cicero's *De Finibus* III; on first movements, Seneca's *On Anger* II.2–4 and Aulus Gellius, *Attic Nights* XIX.1. Hierocles on drawing the circles inward is preserved in Stobaeus. *Prohairesis* is Epictetus' own term (*Discourses* I.1); the doctrine of continuous attention, *prosoche*, underlies the daily practice and is developed in *Discourses* III.12 and IV.12.

No modern psychology appears in this manual. Nothing is claimed here that the ancients did not claim.

Where this book and the masters disagree, this book is wrong. Discard it.

Print it cheap. Use it until worn out. Then transcribe the key lines into your own hand and burn this copy.
