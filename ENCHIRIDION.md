# THE ENCHIRIDION

### The Handbook of Renato Zapata

> *Enchiridion*: Greek — "that which is held in the hand."
> Epictetus' students compiled his handbook
> so a man could carry the teaching into the battle of life.
> This one is yours. Written to yourself.
> Held in your own hand. Opened when it matters.
> It is enough.

---

## THE CARD

*One screen. Ten seconds. The whole book lives in these lines.*

> "There are more things, Lucilius, likely to frighten us
> than there are to crush us; we suffer more often
> in imagination than in reality."
> — Seneca, *Letters* 13.4

Imagination is not evidence.

Some things are yours. Most things are not.
**Not mine. Return.**

The obstacle is the training.

Given back, not lost.

Suffer once, when it happens —
not twice, in advance.

The feast is still on the table.

On fire? Next screen.
Breathe on the way down.

---

## ON FIRE

Find your fire:
**RACING MIND — ANGER — DREAD — CRAVING — GRIEF — PARALYSIS**

Every fire, first, the Four Beats.
One exhale per line. The rhythm is the mnemonic.

1. **BREATHE** — one breath. Longer out than in.
2. **NAME** — say what happened. Only what happened.
3. **SORT** — yours, or not yours?
4. **ACT** — one move. Take it.

Then open your card.

---

### RACING MIND

You will know it by the loop:
the same scene, playing its tenth showing.

1. Ask: is this happening, or imagined?
   Find a fact — something you could photograph.
2. No fact? Say it once, aloud:
   imagination is not evidence.
3. A fact? Shrink it to the next ten minutes.
   One action inside those minutes is yours.
   Do that. Only that.

> "Don't let your imagination be crushed by life as a whole.
> Don't try to picture everything bad that could possibly happen.
> Stick with the situation at hand."
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* 8.36

Storm passed. Name one thing still here.
The feast is still on the table.

---

### ANGER

You will know it by the heat in your face
and the speech already drafting itself.

1. Say nothing for one full breath. Nothing.
2. Ask it plain: what does his error
   cost my character? Nothing — unless I sign.
   His act was never yours. Your answer is.
   **Not mine. Return.**
3. Choose the response you would respect tomorrow.

> "The greatest remedy for anger is delay."
> — Seneca, *On Anger* 2.29

> "The best revenge is not to be like that."
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* 6.6

Storm passed. Look at what he could not touch:
your judgment, your evening, your table. Still yours.

---

### DREAD

You will know it by the funeral you are attending
for something that has not died.

1. Name the feared thing in one plain sentence.
2. Ask: has it happened yet?
   It has not. You are rehearsing.
3. Prepare one action for if it comes.
   Drop the rest.

> "He suffers more than is necessary,
> who suffers before it is necessary."
> — Seneca, *Letters* 98.8

Suffer once, when it happens — not twice, in advance.
Storm passed. The evening you were about to waste
is still here. Spend it.

---

### CRAVING

You will know it by the glow around a thing
that will be ordinary the moment you hold it.
Or by the hand already moving toward the phone.
The scroll is the same fire at lower heat —
a craving for elsewhere. Same drill.

1. Strip it. Describe it plainly,
   no glamour, in ten words.
2. Wait ten minutes.
   Cravings crest and fall. All of them.
3. Ask what the craving is actually asking for.
   Go feed that.
   If the craving is the phone, the answer is usually
   rest, contact, or escape from the task —
   feed the real one, or run the PARALYSIS card
   on the task you are fleeing.

The dish has not yet come to you.
Do not stretch your desire toward it —
wait until it is opposite you.
(after Epictetus, *Enchiridion* 15)

Storm passed. Taste what is already on your plate.

---

### GRIEF

You will know it by the wave.
Let the first wave through.
The tear is not a failure.

1. Stand still until the wave passes through you.
2. Say the oldest words in this book:
   given back, not lost.
3. Do one act of care for someone still here.
   Grief moves when love has somewhere to go.

> "Never say about anything, 'I have lost it,'
> but say, 'I have restored it.'
> Is your child dead? It has been restored.
> Is your wife dead? She has been restored."
> — Epictetus, *Enchiridion* 11

Everything was a loan. You held it well.

---

### PARALYSIS

You will know it by the task
grown larger than the day.

1. Shrink the work to the next physical action.
   Not the project — the keystroke, the phone call, the door.
2. Begin badly. Beginning is the only door there is.
3. Let the blocker become the assignment.

> "The impediment to action advances action.
> What stands in the way becomes the way."
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* 5.20

The obstacle is the training.
Storm passed. Notice: the dread of the work
was heavier than the work.

## THE RETURN

*Read this page when you have skipped days
and the skipping has begun to accuse you.*

You missed a day. Or a week.
Marcus missed days. The *Meditations* exists
because he kept returning to it.
The record was never the practice.
The return is the practice.

The law of this page:

1. Never miss twice. At the next anchor —
   feet on floor, hand on car door, phone on charger —
   take the smallest version. Ten seconds counts.
2. After three missed days: read the Card only,
   plus the ten-second morning line, for three days.
   Ambition at re-entry is how practices die.
3. The skip itself is material.
   Run the Four Beats on the shame.
   The obstacle is the training — even this one.

Do not count streaks. Count returns.
Measure how fast you come back, not how long you last.

**You are the kind of man who comes back.**
And the feast is still on the table.

## THE HAND

*The copy of this book you always carry.
Learn the hand once — this book will speak
to your fingers from now on.
It will open for you in the dark.*

Make a fist. Walk it open, finger by finger.

**Thumb — YOURS?**
The thumb points back at you.
Is this up to me? Judgment, desire, fear, next act — yes.
Everything else — no.

**Index — NAME IT.**
The pointing finger. Describe the thing bare,
no adjectives. An email containing words.
A sensation in the chest. That is all that is certain.

**Middle — THE FEE.**
Traffic on the 410. The rude reply. The delay.
These are the fee for living among humans.
The fee is paid. Drive.

**Ring — REHEARSE THE LOSS.**
The ring marks what you are bound to.
Fifteen seconds: imagine it gone. Then open your eyes —
returned to you, this once. Greet it.
End on the greeting, always.
A rehearsal that ends on the loss
is worry wearing armor.

**Pinky — RETURN.**
The smallest finger, the smallest act.
After any fall: one breath, one line, back on the road.

In crisis: fist, then fingers, one by one.
Run your thumb over the trouble first. Is this yours?
Not yours? Return it — and remember:
the feast is still on the table.

## MORNING

*Full rite: ninety seconds. Not one second more.
If it takes longer than a red light, it will not survive you.*

**The minimum — ten seconds, and it counts.
This is not the consolation prize. This is the practice.**

When your feet touch the floor, before you stand, say:

> Some things are up to me. Most things are not.
> Today I work my side of the line.

**The full rite, when there is slack:**

First, receive the day. Before the phone,
before the coffee, before San Antonio is even awake, say:
*This day is a gift, and I will be at the table for it.*

Second, the emperor's argument with the bed —
he had it too, in the mud of a German winter:

> "At dawn, when you have trouble getting out of bed,
> tell yourself: 'I have to go to work — as a human being.
> What do I have to complain of, if I'm going to do
> what I was born for — the things I was
> brought into the world to do?'"
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* 5.1

Third, the inoculation. Name today's friction before it names you:

> "When you wake up in the morning, tell yourself:
> the people I deal with today will be meddling,
> ungrateful, arrogant, dishonest, jealous, and surly."
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* 2.1

He wrote that and then went out and governed them,
and loved some of them. So will you.
None of it will be new. None of it will be about you.
All of it is material.
You are not predicting disaster.
You are removing its one weapon: surprise.
Rehearsal has a start, a clock, and an ending.
Worry has none of them.

Last — and the morning never ends on threat — gratitude,
with the same rigor as any drill. Abstractions are banned.
Not "my family": the exact person, the exact moment,
something you could have photographed.
Her laugh at the kitchen table. The boy asleep in the truck
on the way home. Name one person. Name one moment
from the last day. Name one thing you use daily
and would crave if it vanished.

> "Do not dream of possession of what you have not:
> rather reckon up the chief of the blessings you do possess,
> and then thankfully remember how you would crave for them
> if they were not yours."
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* 7.27

Then say: all of this is on loan,
and all of it is here this morning.
The feast is still on the table.

Close the rite:

**You are a man who works his side of the line.**

## THE FIRST SIP

*The smallest unit in this book.
If you keep one practice, keep this one.*

The day's first coffee.
Before you drink: three breaths.
Then take the first sip as if it were
the first cup ever poured for you,
or the last you will be given.
Heat. Bitterness. The weight of the cup.
One sip, full attention.
Then the day may proceed.
The feast is still on the table.
This sip was proof.

## THE ANCHORS

*The city will ring the bell for you.
Five objects, five practices, zero willpower —
and one more bell for the decisions that carry weight.*

When your feet touch the floor —
the morning line, before you stand.

When your hand touches the car door handle, say:
the traffic, the heat, the fools — the fee. Already paid.

When you catch yourself rehearsing an argument
that has not happened, say: imagination or reality? —
and run the Four Beats. Every jam on the 410
is a scheduled training session. The city keeps the schedule.

When a decision has weight, ask the four questions:
What here is truly good, truly bad, merely indifferent?
What do I owe the people in this?
What am I avoiding?
What am I overdoing?
Wisdom. Justice. Courage. Temperance.
Four questions, not four posters.

When you cross your own doorway coming home:
one exhale on the threshold. The day stays outside.
Inside is the feast.

When you plug your phone in at night —
the charger is the bell. Three fingers, three questions.
The evening rite begins.

---

## EVENING

*Full rite: three minutes. Mirror of the morning.
Morning prepares. Evening digests.*

**The minimum — thirty seconds, while the phone charges.
Three fingers:**
Thumb — what did I do well?
Index — where did I slip?
Middle — what is tomorrow's one thing?

**The full rite:**

This is the practice Seneca learned from Sextius
and made his own, done nightly,
pleading his cause before his own court:

> "What bad habit have you put right today?
> Which fault did you take a stand against?
> In what respect are you better?"
> — Seneca, *On Anger* 3.36

Answer aloud or on paper. Hide nothing. Pass nothing by.
Then the rule from the same page:
prosecute without cruelty.
See that you don't do it again. Tonight, you are forgiven.

Then two questions the auditors always forget,
and this book does not:

What did I fail to savor today?
Name it. No penance — just notice,
so tomorrow's eyes are open.

What joy was handed to me today?
Name it with a face, a sound, a taste.
The last line before sleep is an inhale.

The day was a life in miniature.
It was frightening in places, and it did not crush you.
Imagination was not evidence. It rarely is.

Close the rite:

**You are a man who examines the day, then drops it.**

## THE DOCTRINE

*The source code of the protocols you have already run.
Read slowly. This is why the cards work.
Every chapter ends where it began: in the fire.*

---

### I. Some things are yours. Most things are not.

> "Some things are in our control and others not."
> — Epictetus, *Enchiridion* 1

The first sentence of the first handbook,
and the cornerstone of everything.
Hear it strictly, because the soft version fails under load.

Yours: what you judge, what you want,
what you fear, what you set out to do.
Four things. All of them inside your skin.

And what should those four things aim at?
The school gave one answer for five centuries:
the only unconditional good is excellence of character,
and it has four faces.
**Wisdom** — seeing what is truly good, bad, indifferent.
**Justice** — giving each person what is owed.
**Courage** — facing what should be faced.
**Temperance** — the right measure, nothing overdone.
Everything else — money, health, the house, the company —
is material for the virtues, never the prize.
This is why the four questions at the weighty-decision anchor
are those four questions and no others.

Not yours: your body, your money,
your name in other mouths, your position —
and every outcome of every act.
Even the body is on the wrong side of the line.
Epictetus, who had been a slave and walked lame, knew.

And reputation — the heaviest of the not-yours —
he would have you set down first:

> "It never ceases to amaze me: we all love ourselves
> more than other people, but care more about
> their opinion than our own."
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* 12.4

Confuse the lists and you will be hindered,
you will grieve, you will blame gods and men.
Keep them straight and no one can coerce you,
no one can hinder you. That is his promise,
and it has held for nineteen centuries.

But you have plans — a company, a family, a house rising.
The Stoic does not abandon aims; he signs them differently.
Every intention carries a silent rider: *fate permitting.*
The sage sails — unless something prevents
(Seneca, *On Tranquility* 13).
You are the archer: draw well, aim true, release clean.
Yours: the aim. Not yours: the landing.
So aim like it is everything,
and release like it is nothing.

**Not mine. Return.**
Say it once, and put your hands back on the work.
What is up to you? Begin there.

In the fire, this becomes: the thumb. *Up to me?*
The feast is still on the table.

---

### II. Imagination is not evidence.

> "Men are disturbed not by the things which happen,
> but by the opinions about the things."
> — Epictetus, *Enchiridion* 5

Here is the machinery under your anchor line.
An impression arrives unbidden — the flash of ruin,
the imagined argument, the jolt in the chest.
The arrival is not the suffering.
Suffering begins when you sign the impression —
when you countersign "this is a catastrophe"
and make it policy.

The flinch is nature. The signature is you.
Stoicism begins one heartbeat after the flinch.
Even the sage startles; even Seneca's heart leaped.
You are not failing when you feel.
You are practicing when you pause before you sign.

So the drill is always the same.
Strip the event to what a camera would show.
There is an email containing these words.
There is a number on a screen.
Everything else — the doom, the verdict, the ending —
is commentary, and you are the one adding it.

And when the impression is an insult, a slight, a wrong —
the same gate holds:

> "Choose not to be harmed — and you won't feel harmed.
> Don't feel harmed — and you haven't been."
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* 4.7

You suffered the meeting twice:
once in your head, once in the room.
Cancel the first showing.

Suffer once, when it happens — not twice, in advance.

In the fire, this becomes: **RACING MIND**.
*Something you could photograph.*
And when the fire is another man: **ANGER**.
*Nothing — unless I sign.*
The feast is still on the table.

### Rehearse the winter.

> "He suffers more than is necessary,
> who suffers before it is necessary."
> — Seneca, *Letters* 98.8

Then why does this book order you to imagine
the rude colleague, the loss, the fee?
Because rehearsal and worry are different machines
that use the same images.
Worry is involuntary, endless, and stops at the disaster.
Rehearsal is **chosen** — you set the appointment;
**timed** — fifteen seconds, sixty, never open-ended;
and it **ends at your response** — the last frame
is always you, acting well, or the loved thing
returned to you this once.
Run the images on your schedule
and they lose the power to run on theirs.
You are not predicting disaster.
You are removing its one weapon: surprise.

In the fire, this becomes: the morning inoculation,
and the Ring finger.
*A rehearsal that ends on the loss is worry wearing armor.*

---

### III. The three disciplines.

Epictetus split the whole training in three,
and every card in this book is one of them, running.

**Desire** — want only what is yours;
aim the reaching at what cannot be confiscated.
This is Chapter VI and the CRAVING card.

**Action** — act with the silent rider, *fate permitting*;
the archer's clean release.
This is Chapter I and the PARALYSIS card.

**Assent** — sign no impression unexamined.
This is Chapter II and the RACING MIND card.

When a day goes wrong, one of the three slipped.
Find which. That is the diagnosis;
the cards are the cure.

In the fire, this becomes: the Four Beats —
SORT is desire, ACT is action, NAME is assent.

---

### IV. The obstacle is the training.

> "The impediment to action advances action.
> What stands in the way becomes the way."
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* 5.20

A wrestler does not resent his opponent;
the opponent is the practice.
Fire makes flame and brightness
out of everything thrown into it.
The blocked plan is not the end of the work —
it is the next assignment, delivered early.

Marcus did not write this in a study.
He wrote it in the mud of a German winter,
in a plague, at the head of an army he never wanted,
to himself, at night.

And do not wait for the universe to explain itself.
He gave you the hedge that makes this weatherproof:
providence or atoms. Either the cosmos is ordered
or it is chance — and either way your task is identical:
judge clearly, act justly, accept what you cannot change.
The practice asks the cosmos for nothing.
That is why it holds when the cosmos gives nothing.

As for the people who obstruct you —
the client who will not answer, the driver on the 410,
the man who failed you — Marcus met them daily
and wrote the verdict once: we were made for cooperation,
like feet, like hands, like rows of teeth.
Another limb of your own body is in your way.
Anger at him is the hand hating the foot.

The obstacle is the training.
It never stops being the training.

In the fire, this becomes: **PARALYSIS**.
*The next physical action.*
The feast is still on the table.

---

### V. Given back, not lost.

Everything you love is on loan.
The company. The house. The people at your table.
This is not a threat. It is the price tag,
and reading it correctly is the beginning of joy.

> "Ask not that events should happen as you will,
> but let your will be that events should happen as they do,
> and you shall have peace."
> — Epictetus, *Enchiridion* 8

A later age gave this a Latin tag: *amor fati*.
The tag is modern. The practice is ancient.
Not resignation — embrace.
The good flow of life
comes to the man who stops arm-wrestling the river.

And because everything is on loan,
love harder now, not softer.
The Stoics kissed their children goodnight
with a whisper that would break a lesser school:

> "As you kiss your son good night, says Epictetus,
> whisper to yourself, 'He may be dead in the morning.'"
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* 11.34

Not morbid arithmetic. The only reliable technology
for undivided attention ever discovered.
So, at every goodbye, every goodnight, every hang-up
with someone you love, one silent sentence:
*Mortal. Therefore precious. Therefore now.*
Then give the parting your whole face.
No phone in the hand during goodbyes.
If you parted badly with anyone today,
repair it before sleep. The whisper makes grudges expensive.

Given back, not lost. You held it well.
Hold it well tonight.

In the fire, this becomes: **GRIEF**.
*Given back, not lost.*

---

### VI. The feast.

Understand the goal, or the armor is wasted.
The end of all this discipline is not hardness.
Grim endurance is not mastery.
It is armor worn to bed.
The end is joy — solid, load-bearing joy,
standing on ground that cannot be taken.

> "Real joy, believe me, is a stern matter."
> — Seneca, *Letters* 23.4

*Disce gaudere*, he told Lucilius. Learn joy.
An order, not a wish — because joy is a skill,
and like any skill it rots without reps.

The school had two words for the prize.
*Gaudium* — joy proper, the deep-running kind
that does not depend on delivery trucks.
And *euthymia* — Seneca borrowed it from Democritus
in *On Tranquility of Mind* 2: the steady well-being
of a soul that trusts its own path
and does not glance sideways at anyone else's.
Not a mood. A ground.

Pleasure is on loan from outside:
the promotion, the praise, the drink. It spikes and leaves,
and whoever issued the loan can call it in.
Joy rests on what is yours outright:
your judgment, your character,
this present hour rightly received.
Test everything you chase with one question:
can another man's decision take this from me?
If yes — enjoy it lightly, as a guest enjoys the wine.
If no — build on it.

This is why craving lies to you.
Epictetus made desire the first discipline
because it is the first thing to slip its leash.
The glow around the not-yet-held thing is borrowed light.
You have held enough trophies to know:
each turned ordinary within a month of arrival.
The glow was never in the thing.
It was in the reaching.
Retrain the reaching — aim it at what
cannot be confiscated — and the glow
settles over your own table.

And keep death on the table, where the Stoics kept it.
Death is not the dark chapter of this book.
It is the seasoning in every other one.

> "Think of yourself as dead. You have lived your life.
> Now take what's left and live it properly."
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* 7.56

> "Not to live as if you had endless years ahead of you.
> Death overshadows you. While you're alive and able —
> be good."
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* 4.17

This is what makes the wine taste like wine
and the people at your table irreplaceable tonight.
Once a day, sixty seconds, run the Golden Hour:
stop. Look at the ordinary scene in front of you —
the kitchen, the truck cab, the office at dusk,
the last hour of Texas light.
Now see it from ten years ahead, as memory:
an evening you would pay anything to stand inside again.
Say: I am inside it now. Then resume.

> "It is not that we have a short time to live,
> but that we waste a lot of it."
> — Seneca, *On the Shortness of Life* 1.3

You are not being asked to feel less.
You are being trained to stop paying imaginary debts,
so that what you feel is finally about what is real.
The steel exists to protect the feast.
A man trains so that nothing can take the feast from him.

The feast is still on the table. Sit down to it.

In the fire, this becomes: **CRAVING**.
*The dish not yet opposite you.*

---

### VII. The view from above.

Some days one trouble fills the whole sky.
Understand: that is not the trouble's size.
That is how close you are standing to it.

> "Look down from above on the countless herds of men
> and their countless solemnities, and the infinitely
> varied voyagings in storms and calms,
> and the differences among those who are born,
> who live together, and die."
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* 9.30

Sixty seconds. Climb.
This room. This building. San Antonio,
a grid of light on dark land. Texas.
The curve of the planet, turning.
The whole length of the timeline —
every man who ever sweated a deadline,
buried a friend, feared a Tuesday.
Now find your afternoon down there.
Find the email.

Not escape. Scale correction.
The trouble keeps its true size —
which was never the size of the sky.

Then descend.
And do the next right thing at full size.

In the fire, this becomes: any card,
when the problem fills the sky.
*Sixty seconds up. Then down. Then act.*
The feast is still on the table.

## THE LADDER

*Do not climb faster than the rungs appear.
The test at every phase: did the practice fire
before you remembered to do it?
If yes, add the next piece. If not, stay.*

**Week 1 — the seed.**
The ten-second morning line, feet on floor.
One question at night, phone on charger:
what did I do well?
Nothing else. Success is five days of seven.
Do not read past the Card and the ON FIRE pages.
Let them get heavy in the hand.

**Week 2 — the Beats.**
Add the Four Beats, and one Spiral Drill daily:
sixty seconds — deliberately summon a real, current worry
and run the Beats on it. Simulated fire
is the only rehearsal that transfers to real fire.

**Weeks 3 and 4 — the rites.**
Add the full morning rite and the three-finger evening.
Add the premeditation: today you will meet
the rude, the slow, the dishonest — as you always have.
Add the First Sip.

**Month 2 — the Hand.**
Learn all five fingers. Run one live problem
through the thumb daily, on paper.
One chosen discomfort weekly — the cold shower,
the skipped meal, the hard conversation, the plain fare —
picked in advance at Sunday's review.

> "Set aside a certain number of days, during which
> you shall be content with the scantiest and cheapest fare,
> with coarse and rough dress, saying to yourself the while:
> 'Is this the condition that I feared?'"
> — Seneca, *Letters* 18.5–6

The rule: chosen, bounded, and ended on the savor beat —
the first warm water, the first full meal, named aloud.
The drill teaches two things in one motion:
that you can lose the comfort, and how good it still is.
A discomfort that ends in grimness is armor worn to bed.
Add the Golden Hour and the Ring drill:
fifteen seconds of imagined loss, never more,
always ending in the greeting. The savor beat is mandatory.
A rehearsal that ends on the loss is worry wearing armor.

**Month 3 — the voice.**
The three identity lines, verbatim, at their stations.
Teach one line of this book to another person weekly —
what you can explain, you own.
Monthly Cold Read: write the Card, the Beats,
and the five fingers from memory.
Whatever your hand has forgotten
goes back into the daily drill.
The book demotes itself to reference.
The Hand carries the practice now.
The ladder does not end in mastery.
It ends in mornings you no longer need it.

**The only two numbers that matter:**
Catches — each time you noticed the spiral
and ran even one Beat. Tally them Sunday night.
Return speed — after any miss, how many anchors passed
before you were back. Fewer each month.

Past seven days, stop counting streaks.
Chains are for prisoners.

> "How long will you wait
> to think yourself worthy of the best things? ...
> From now on, then, resolve to live
> as a grown-up who is making progress."
> — Epictetus, *Enchiridion* 51

> "Lay hold of to-day's task, and you will not need
> to depend so much upon to-morrow's.
> While we are postponing, life speeds by."
> — Seneca, *Letters* 1.2

What is up to you? Begin there.
Begin today, at the smallest rung.
The feast is still on the table.

## THE CREED

*First person. Aloud. By day thirty, from memory.
Recited, never skimmed.*

I hold what is mine: my judgment, my aim, my next act.
I release what is not: the outcome, the verdict, the past.
I will not sign every impression that knocks.
I will meet the difficult as material, the obstacle as training.
I will pay the fee without complaint; I was told the price.
I will love what is mortal harder for being mortal.
I will take the dish as it passes, and not clutch as it goes.
I will examine the day each night, then forgive it, then drop it.
I will return after every fall, at the smallest step, without ceremony.
I will keep death in view so that today tastes like something.
I will learn joy, and build it on ground no one can take.
I will guard the feast with steel, and sit down to it laughing.
I will not suffer in imagination what I need not suffer in reality.

## SOURCES

No quote appears in this handbook without a citation.

Seneca, *Letters to Lucilius* 13.4 (trans. Gummere, Loeb 1917) —
"Plura sunt, Lucili, quae nos terrent quam quae premunt,
et saepius opinione quam re laboramus."
The popular version drops the first clause;
the full sentence is stronger.
Also *Letters* 1.2, 18.5–6, 23.4, 57 (the darkness
of the Naples tunnel — the jolt even the trained heart feels),
98.8; *On Anger* 2.29, 3.36 (the nightly audit,
reporting the practice of Sextius);
*On Tranquility of Mind* 2 (euthymia, borrowed
from Democritus), 13; *On the Shortness of Life* 1.3
(trans. Costa, Penguin).

Epictetus, *Enchiridion* 1, 5, 8, 11, 15, 51
(trans. Long, Matheson, Carter as noted in tradition);
*Discourses* 1.16 (trans. Long).

Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* 2.1, 4.7, 4.17, 5.1, 5.20,
6.6, 7.27, 7.56, 8.36, 9.30, 11.34, 12.4
(trans. Hays, except 7.27, trans. Farquharson,
and 9.30, trans. Long).
In 11.34 Marcus is quoting Epictetus
(cf. *Discourses* 3.24).

Where this book compresses rather than quotes —
the archer (Cicero, *On Ends* 3.22), first movements
(Seneca, *On Anger* 2.4), providence or atoms
(Marcus 4.3, 9.28), the wrestler and the fire
(Marcus 7.61, 4.1), cooperation like feet and hands
(Marcus 2.1), the dish not yet come
(Epictetus, *Enchiridion* 15, on the CRAVING card),
death kept daily in view
(after Epictetus, *Enchiridion* 21) — it says so.
"Amor fati" is a later Latin tag, Nietzsche's,
for the ancient practice of *Enchiridion* 8.

The term *enchiridion* is Epictetus' own editor's:
the handbook, the thing held in the hand.
Everything else in these pages
is your own voice, trained.
This is yours. Carry it.

## THE LAST PAGE

One image, and you may close the book.

> "Remember that in life you ought to behave
> as at a banquet. Suppose that something is carried round
> and is opposite to you. Stretch out your hand
> and take a portion with decency.
> Suppose that it passes by you. Do not detain it.
> Suppose that it is not yet come to you.
> Do not send your desire forward to it,
> but wait till it is opposite to you."
> — Epictetus, *Enchiridion* 15

That is the whole art. You are at the feast.
The bell will ring some days.
You have your cards, your Hand, your Beats.
You will stand. You will handle it.
You will sit back down to the table.
The armor was always for this:
so that nothing could take the feast from you.

The other master, sold young and enslaved,
lame in one leg, ended his days singing:

> "If I were a nightingale, I would do the part
> of a nightingale; if I were a swan, the part of a swan.
> But now I am a rational creature,
> and I ought to praise God: this is my work;
> I do it, nor will I desert this post,
> so long as I am allowed to keep it;
> and I exhort you to join in this same song."
> — Epictetus, *Discourses* 1.16

He was not enduring. He was rejoicing, at his post.
Both are one motion. That is the secret
the whole book has been repeating.

And your anchor line, the one that brought you here —
read it one last time, then live its answer:

Most of what frightened you never came.
What came, you carried.
**Reality, when it comes,
is smaller than you imagined it.
The feast is not.**
And the table is longer than you feared,
and the people at it are still here,
and the light through the window is the same light
that fell on Rome.

Some things are up to you. Most things are not.
Today, work your side of the line.
Tonight, sit down to the feast.

**You are the kind of man who comes back.**
